Self-manage or hire a property manager?

Should you self-manage your rental or hire a property manager? What managers charge, what you get for it, and how to price your own time honestly.

By Denver McClure, founder of Apollo Advising. Updated October 12, 2026.

Short answer. For a long-term rental, managers commonly charge around 8% to 10% of collected rent plus a leasing fee for each new tenant. Divide that annual cost by the hours you would actually spend, and you have the hourly rate you are paying yourself to self-manage. If you live far away, own several properties, or hate the work, a good manager is usually worth it.

What managers usually charge

FeeCommon rangeNotes
Monthly managementAbout 8% to 10% of collected rent for long-term rentalsShort-term rental managers often charge 20% to 35% of revenue
Leasing feeOften 50% to 100% of one month's rentCharged when a new tenant signs

Maintenance markupSometimes 10% on vendor invoicesAsk whether they use in-house crews
Setup and vacancy feesVariesRead the contract for these

Fees vary by market and company. The contract terms matter as much as the percentages: how you can cancel, who chooses vendors, what repair amount they can approve without you, and how quickly they pay you.

Price your own time

On a $2,200 a month rental, a 9% fee is about $2,376 a year. A leasing fee of 75% of a month's rent every 2 years adds about $825 a year, for about $3,201 in total.

If self-managing takes you about 4 hours a month plus 20 hours each time a tenant turns over, that is roughly 58 hours a year. You would be paying yourself about $55 an hour to do it. Whether that is a good trade depends on what else your time is worth and how much you mind 9 p.m. phone calls.

Self-managing tends to work when

  • You live within a reasonable drive of the property
  • You have reliable vendors and a system for rent, repairs, and records
  • You know the landlord-tenant rules in your state and city, including deposits, notices, and fair housing
  • You have one to a few properties and the time to handle them

A manager tends to win when

  • You are out of state or travel often
  • You run a short-term rental and do not want a part-time job
  • You are stepping back in retirement
  • You would otherwise defer maintenance or skip proper tenant screening

Questions to ask a manager

  • How many doors does each property manager handle?
  • How do you screen tenants, and what are your eviction and turnover rates?
  • What can you approve without me, and how do you choose vendors?
  • How and when do I get paid, and what reports do I get?
  • In Texas, is the company licensed with the Texas Real Estate Commission? Managing and leasing property for others generally requires a real estate license.
