Return on equity: the rental number most owners never check

How to calculate return on equity for a rental, why it drifts down as a property appreciates, and what it does and does not tell you about selling.

By Denver McClure, founder of Apollo Advising. Updated October 12, 2026.

Short answer. Return on equity is a rental's annual net cash flow divided by your equity, which is the market value minus what you owe. A rental worth $450,000 with a $150,000 loan that nets $9,000 a year is earning 3% on its $300,000 of equity, before appreciation and loan paydown.

The calculation

Equity is what the property would sell for today minus the loan balance. Net cash flow is a year of rent minus every operating expense, the full mortgage payment, and a reserve for repairs and capital items like roofs and HVAC. Divide the second number by the first.

Use today's value, not what you paid. Return on equity asks what the money tied up in the property is earning now, which is a different question from how good the purchase was.

Why the number drifts down

As a property appreciates and the loan is paid down, equity usually grows faster than rent. A rental that earned double digits on its down payment in year one can earn under 4% on its equity fifteen years later, even while its cash flow more than doubles. Nothing went wrong. The money simply piled up inside the property.

That is why long-time owners are often surprised by this number, and why it is the first thing we look at in a hold or sell conversation.

What it leaves out

Return on equity is a cash-flow measure. It does not include:

  • Appreciation, which may or may not continue in your market
  • Loan paydown, the part of each mortgage payment that builds equity
  • Tax effects such as depreciation, which your CPA can quantify
  • What selling would cost: commissions, closing costs, and taxes on the gain, including depreciation recapture

So a low number is a reason to look closer, not a verdict. A full analysis puts cash flow, paydown, appreciation, and the cost of selling into one picture.

What owners do with it

Once you know the number, the realistic paths are usually these: keep the property as it is, raise its income with a strategy change, pull equity out with a refinance or HELOC, sell it, or exchange it under Section 1031. Each one changes cash flow, risk, taxes, and the time the property takes.

Whether 3% is enough depends on your goals, your taxes, your time, and what else the money would do. Comparing that number against stocks, bonds, or other securities is investment advice, which Apollo Advising does not provide.
