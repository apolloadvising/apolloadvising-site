How to analyze a rental property before you buy

How to analyze a rental property before you buy: cap rate, cash-on-cash return, DSCR, and the expense lines first-time buyers leave out, with a worked example.

By Denver McClure, founder of Apollo Advising. Updated October 12, 2026.

Short answer. Start with net operating income: a year of realistic rent, minus vacancy and every operating cost, before the mortgage. Divide it by the price for the cap rate, subtract the loan payments for cash flow, and divide cash flow by the cash you put in for cash-on-cash return. Most bad purchases come from leaving costs out, not from the math.

Step 1: realistic income

Use rent for comparable homes that actually leased recently, not the seller's pro forma or the highest listing you can find. Then take out vacancy. Even a well-run long-term rental sits empty between tenants; 5% to 8% of gross rent is a common planning range.

Step 2: every operating cost

This is where first-time buyers go wrong. Include:

  • Property tax, based on what the purchase price will likely do to the assessed value, not the seller's current bill
  • Insurance, quoted for a landlord policy at this address
  • Maintenance and a reserve for capital items such as roofs, HVAC, and water heaters. See how to budget for them
  • Property management, even if you plan to self-manage, because your time has a cost. See the management guide
  • HOA dues, lawn, pest, pool, and any utilities the owner pays
  • Leasing and turnover costs: cleaning, paint, and listing fees

Step 3: the three numbers

MetricHow to calculateWhat it tells you
Cap rateNet operating income ÷ priceWhat the property earns on its price, before financing. Useful for comparing properties.
Cash-on-cash returnAnnual cash flow after the mortgage ÷ cash investedWhat your down payment and closing costs earn in cash each year.
DSCRNet operating income ÷ annual mortgage paymentsHow comfortably the income covers the debt. Lenders look at this.

A worked example

A $320,000 house renting for $2,400 a month, bought with 25% down and a 30-year loan at 7.0%. Illustrative numbers, not a forecast for any market.

LineAnnual
Gross rent$28,800
Less vacancy at 5%−$1,440
Property tax−$7,200
Insurance−$2,600
Maintenance 10%, capital reserve 5%, management 8% of rent−$6,624
Net operating income$10,936
Mortgage payments−$19,161
Cash flow−$8,225

That is a cap rate of 3.4%, a DSCR of 0.57, and cash-on-cash of -9.2% on about $89,000 invested. In other words, this house costs money to hold every year at today's rates, even though the rent looks strong at first glance. That does not automatically make it a bad purchase, since loan paydown and appreciation also count, but you should know the number going in and decide whether you can carry it.

Shortcuts to treat with caution

  • The 1% rule (monthly rent at least 1% of price) is a quick screen, not an analysis. It ignores taxes, insurance, and condition, which vary enormously.
  • Seller pro formas often leave out management, reserves, and vacancy.
  • Short-term rental projections from data tools can be wildly optimistic for a specific house. Treat them as a ceiling.
