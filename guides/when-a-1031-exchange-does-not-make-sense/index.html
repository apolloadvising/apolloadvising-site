When a 1031 exchange does not make sense

A 1031 exchange defers tax, but it is not always the best move. Six situations where paying the tax can beat deferring it, and how to run the comparison.

By Denver McClure, founder of Apollo Advising. Updated October 12, 2026.

Short answer. A 1031 exchange is worth it when the tax you defer is large and you genuinely want the replacement property. It often is not worth it when the gain is small, when you want out of real estate, when the only replacements available are weak, or when the deadlines would push you into overpaying. Compare the after-tax result of selling with the realistic result of the exchange.

Deferring tax is valuable, but a 1031 exchange comes with costs and constraints: an intermediary fee, a 45-day identification deadline, a 180-day closing deadline, and the requirement to buy property of equal or greater value with all of the proceeds. Those constraints are worth accepting in some situations and not in others.

Six situations where selling can win

  1. The gain is small. If the tax owed is modest, the fees, rushed timeline, and restrictions can cost more than the deferral is worth.
  2. You want out of real estate. An exchange keeps your money in property. If what you want is simplicity or cash, deferring the tax only postpones the same decision, often to a point when you have less energy for it.
  3. The replacement would be weak. Buying a mediocre property to save tax is a common and expensive mistake. A bad property can lose more than the tax you saved.
  4. The clock would force a bad price. In tight markets, buyers with a 45-day deadline overpay. If you would not buy the property without the tax benefit, that is a warning sign.
  5. Your tax rate is low this year. In a low-income year, such as the first year of retirement, part of the gain may be taxed at a lower rate. Your CPA can estimate this.
  6. You need cash. Any cash you take out of an exchange is generally taxable anyway, so a partial exchange can deliver less benefit than expected.

The comparison to run

Put two columns side by side, over the same holding period:

Sell and pay the taxExchange
Money you keep workingNet proceeds after taxFull proceeds, inside real estate
What it can go intoAnything you chooseLike-kind real property only
Time and effortEnds at closingA new property to buy, finance, and run
RiskDepends on what you do nextConcentrated in the replacement property
Tax laterAlready paidDeferred, and still owed if you later sell without exchanging

If the exchange still wins after you account for the property you would actually buy, it is probably the right move. The 1031 timeline guide covers the rules, and the recapture guide shows how to estimate the tax you would be deferring.

What about a DST?

Some owners who want to stop managing property consider exchanging into a Delaware Statutory Trust. A DST interest is a security with its own risks, fees, and long holding periods.
